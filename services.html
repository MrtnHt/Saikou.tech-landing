How an engagement works

  1. Scope in writing. What is in, what is out, what it costs, when it is delivered.
  2. Authorisation before anything else. A signed agreement that you own or may test what is in scope. For scans this is a hard stop, not a formality.
  3. Your code stays with me and my tools. The verification engine runs locally and needs no network. Nothing is sent to an AI provider unless you agree to it in writing, and then only to the provider you name.
  4. Report and hand-over. You get the findings, the evidence and the limits. Questions afterwards are part of the price.

What you will not get

  • A certificate, a score, or a statement that you are “NIS2 compliant”. Compliance is a legal judgement; this is evidence for it.
  • Legal advice. For that, ask a lawyer.
  • A guarantee that nothing was missed. Every report states what was checked and how, and anything not listed was not tested.

The scope agreement I would ask you to sign is public: docs/business (English and Dutch, a draft that should be reviewed by a lawyer before use).

Why NIS2 makes this matter

The Dutch Cyberbeveiligingswet, which implements the EU’s NIS2 directive, has applied since 15 August 2026 to more than 8,000 organisations in the Netherlands (NCSC).

Article 21(2)(e) of the directive lists, among the required measures, security in the acquisition, development and maintenance of network and information systems, including vulnerability handling and disclosure (EUR-Lex 2022/2555). When AI agents write part of that code, “the agent said it fixed it” is a weak answer to “how do you know?”. An independent check, with its limits written down, is a stronger one.

This is context, not legal advice. Whether and how the law applies to your organisation is a question for you and your lawyer.
